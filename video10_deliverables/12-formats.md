# One post → twelve formats

The file from the video. Twelve derivative formats, each with the rule the agent follows and a
worked example cut from the same source post.

The source post is [`example-post.md`](example-post.md) — *"Support Tickets Are Docs Bugs"*,
published at `fieldnotes.example/support-tickets-are-docs-bugs`. Every example below is derived
from that one article. Nothing here is a summary of a summary: each format goes back to the
original.

**Swap in your platforms.** The file says `<PLATFORM A>` and `<PLATFORM B>` rather than naming
products, because the tone rules matter and the logos do not. Fill those two slots with wherever
you actually publish, and the rest of the file works unchanged.

---

## How to read the rules

Each format has three lines the agent is held to:

- **Shaped for** — where the piece will live, which decides the length and the register.
- **Rule** — the transformation. This is the part that stops twelve pieces from being one piece
  pasted twelve times.
- **Reject if** — the failure mode specific to this format. The review queue checks this.

---

## 1 · Short post — `<PLATFORM A>`

**Shaped for** a fast feed, read on a phone, competing with everything else on the screen.
**Rule** one idea, first-person, opening line under twelve words, no preamble, line breaks doing
the work of punctuation.
**Reject if** it opens by announcing what it is about ("A quick thought on documentation…").

> We read six months of support tickets in one sitting.
>
> We went looking for the product's weak spots. A ranked list of broken things.
>
> Half of them weren't bugs in the product. They were docs bugs.
>
> The answer was written. Published. Live. The person just never found it — while looking in the
> right place.
>
> We had a docs backlog before this. It was a list of pages we felt bad about. Not a list of
> pages that had demonstrably failed.

## 2 · Long post — `<PLATFORM B>`

**Shaped for** a slower, professional feed where the reader will give you six paragraphs if the
first one earns it.
**Rule** keep the argument's structure, add the reasoning the short version had to drop, land on
a transferable method rather than a story.
**Reject if** it reads as the short post with filler between the lines.

> **Six months of tickets, read in one sitting, and what they turned out to be about**
>
> We printed two thousand three hundred support tickets and read them in a day. We expected a
> ranked list of product defects. Half of what we found was not about the product at all.
>
> They were bugs in the documentation — and not the kind where the page is missing. The answer
> was written, published and live, and the person filing the ticket had failed to find it while
> looking in roughly the right place.
>
> That distinction is the whole finding. A ticket about a documented behaviour is a report that a
> specific page failed to answer a specific question at a specific moment. It carries a
> reproduction case, a severity, and a person who will tell you what they were trying to do. It
> is a bug report that happens to arrive in a queue nobody treats as a bug tracker.
>
> The second half explains the first. Nobody reads documentation the way you read a manual. They
> search it, nine minutes into something not working, with three or four words that are not our
> words. We had a page titled *Configuring inbound routing rules*; the tickets it should have
> answered said "emails going to the wrong inbox". The page was correct, complete, well
> organised, and invisible.
>
> The method transfers to any queue you have not been reading as data. Tag tickets against the
> page that should have answered them. Rank pages by tickets rather than traffic — traffic tells
> you what people found, tickets tell you what they needed and didn't get. Then retitle in the
> reader's words and move the answer into the first paragraph, where the six seconds are.

## 3 · Newsletter block

**Shaped for** a section inside a longer letter, read by people who already subscribed and do
not need to be convinced to keep reading.
**Rule** **a fresh opening line** — the newsletter never reuses the article's first sentence,
because a subscriber who has read the post should still find something new in the first three
seconds. Enter through a different door: an aside, a cost, a confession.
**Reject if** the first line is the article's first line, reworded.

> **The rule that did most of the work fits in four words**
>
> Two asks? First paragraph.
>
> That is the whole thing. For each of our twenty worst-performing docs pages we found the two
> questions its tickets actually asked, and put both answers in the opening paragraph — not in a
> summary box, not after the prerequisites, not below the conceptual overview.
>
> The reason it works is unflattering to how we had been writing. A person arrives at a docs page
> mid-panic, from a search box, and gives it about six seconds. Everything we had been putting
> above the answer was written for a reader who does not exist.
>
> We moved three answers up on the first afternoon. Ticket volume on those three pages fell
> inside a week, before we had rewritten a single sentence of the body.
>
> *Full write-up on the blog, including the tagging step that makes the ranking possible.*

## 4 · Quote card — the lesson

**Shaped for** a still image, read in under two seconds, sharable without the article.
**Rule** the line has to survive alone. Pick a sentence with a claim in it, never a sentence that
depends on the paragraph above it.
**Reject if** it needs the article to make sense, or if it is advice rather than an observation.

> **"Every ticket is a docs bug report."**
>
> — Support Tickets Are Docs Bugs

---

## 5 · Quote card — the reframe

**Shaped for** the same slot, chosen for a different reader: the one who agrees there is a
problem and wants the shift in thinking.
**Rule** a before/after in one sentence. Three quote cards from one article must argue three
different things, or you have made one card three times.
**Reject if** it makes the same point as card 4.

> **"People don't read documentation. They search it, mid-panic, with the wrong words."**
>
> — Support Tickets Are Docs Bugs

---

## 6 · Quote card — the result

**Shaped for** the reader who is already convinced and needs the sentence that makes the case
to somebody else.
**Rule** the outcome stated as a mechanism, not as a metric. A number on a card is a claim the
card cannot support.
**Reject if** it contains a figure that the article did not earn.

> **"Same docs. Fewer tickets."**
>
> — Support Tickets Are Docs Bugs

---

## 7 · Thread — five posts

**Shaped for** sequential reading, where each post has to pull the next one.
**Rule** one claim per post, five posts, no padding. The claim in each post has to be able to
stand on its own line; the last post answers the first.
**Reject if** any post is a restatement rather than a new claim, or if a post exists to set up
the next one.

> **1/5** We read six months of support tickets in one sitting. Here's what we found.
>
> **2/5** Half of them weren't bugs in the product. They were bugs in the docs.
>
> **3/5** People don't read documentation. They search it, mid-panic, with the wrong words.
>
> **4/5** So we retitled pages in the reader's words and moved the answer into the first
> paragraph. *Configuring inbound routing rules* became *Mail is going to the wrong inbox*.
>
> **5/5** Ticket volume on the top twenty pages fell by a third in a quarter. We didn't write a
> single new page. Same docs. Fewer tickets.

## 8 · Short-form video hook — 30 seconds

**Shaped for** sound-on vertical video where the first two seconds decide everything.
**Rule** open on the number or the contradiction, never on context. Spoken rhythm, short
sentences, one visual beat per line.
**Reject if** the first sentence contains the word "so" or any setup clause.

> Two thousand three hundred support tickets. Printed. Read in one day.
>
> Half of them weren't product bugs. They were docs bugs.
>
> Not missing pages. The answer was live the whole time — nobody could find it.
>
> Because nobody reads documentation. They search it, mid-panic, with the wrong words.
>
> We changed the titles to the words people actually type. Ticket volume dropped a third. We
> didn't write anything new.

## 9 · Community answer

**Shaped for** a thread where somebody asked a question and a self-promotional answer gets
buried.
**Rule** answer the question first, in the first line. Give the method away in full. Link only
if the article adds something the answer left out.
**Reject if** it opens with credentials or ends with a pitch.

> **Q: How do you decide which docs pages to rewrite first?**
>
> Rank them by support tickets, not by traffic. Traffic tells you what people found. Tickets tell
> you what they needed and didn't get.
>
> The step that makes this possible is tagging: every ticket gets the URL of the page that should
> have answered it. It took us two weeks and it is the only expensive part. Without it you are
> guessing which pages are failing, and everyone guesses their favourites.
>
> What it surfaced for us: our most-read page generated almost no tickets, and our fourth-most-read
> page generated more than the next six combined. No dashboard we owned had ever pointed at it.
>
> Then two cheap fixes per page. Retitle in the words the tickets actually use — ours said "emails
> going to the wrong inbox", our page said "configuring inbound routing rules". And move the two
> most-asked answers into the first paragraph. We moved three answers up on the first afternoon
> and the tickets on those pages fell within a week.

## 10 · Carousel — six slides

**Shaped for** a swipeable set, where a slide is one sentence and a reader decides at slide two.
**Rule** slide one is the tension, slides two through five are the method, slide six is the
transferable rule. One idea per slide, no slide that exists to look full.
**Reject if** any slide requires the previous one to be understood.

> **1** We read six months of support tickets looking for product bugs. Half of them weren't.
>
> **2** They were docs bugs — pages that existed, were correct, and were never found.
>
> **3** Tag every ticket with the page that should have answered it. Two weeks. The only
> expensive step.
>
> **4** Rank pages by tickets, not traffic. Traffic is what people found. Tickets are what they
> needed.
>
> **5** Retitle in the reader's words. Move the two most-asked answers into the first paragraph.
>
> **6** A support queue is a feedback channel already open, already full, already staffed — and
> usually pointed at the wrong department.

## 11 · Objection snippet

**Shaped for** the reply you paste when somebody pushes back, in a support thread or a sales
call.
**Rule** state the objection in the sceptic's own words and concede the strongest part of it
before answering.
**Reject if** it argues with a weaker version of the objection than a real person would raise.

> **"Support tickets are a biased sample. You'll end up optimising docs for the people who
> complain."**
>
> That is right, and it is the strongest version of the objection: everyone who solved their
> problem is invisible in that queue, so the ranking over-weights whatever frustrates the loudest
> users.
>
> Two things keep it usable anyway. First, we are not ranking importance, we are ranking
> *demonstrated failure* — a ticket is proof that one specific page did not work for one specific
> person, which is more than any of our previous prioritisation had. Second, the bias runs
> toward the panicked reader, and the panicked reader is the one our docs were worst for. That is
> the sample we wanted.
>
> Where it does mislead: pages used by people who will never file a ticket — evaluators,
> integrators on someone else's account. Those stay on the qualitative list, and we say so rather
> than pretending the ranking covers them.

## 12 · Internal note

**Shaped for** the team, in a doc or a channel, where the reader wants the decision and the
reasoning behind it.
**Rule** lead with the decision, then the evidence, then what it changes for other channels.
Written for somebody who will have to defend it in a meeting you are absent from.
**Reject if** it reads like the public version with the marketing removed — the internal version
carries the trade-off the public version smoothed over.

> **Decision:** support tickets are now tagged against the docs page that should have answered
> them, and the docs backlog is ranked by that count rather than by traffic or by team opinion.
>
> **Why:** a read-through of six months of tickets found that roughly half concerned documented
> behaviour — the page existed and was correct. Our fourth-most-read page produced more tickets
> than the next six combined and had never appeared on any prioritisation list we kept.
>
> **The trade-off we accepted:** the tagging costs support about a minute per ticket and it is a
> real tax on a queue that is already the busiest thing we own. It is also a biased sample —
> silent readers and evaluators never appear in it. We are accepting a biased ranking over an
> unmeasured one, and the qualitative list stays for the audiences the queue cannot see. If the
> per-ticket cost starts showing up in response times, sampling one day in five is the first
> lever, not dropping the tag.
>
> **What it changes elsewhere:** the same read applies to in-app help text and to the onboarding
> checklist. The question that did the work was "which page should have answered this", and
> nothing about it is specific to documentation.

## Running this yourself

The agent reads the source post once and produces all twelve in a single pass, then writes them
into a review queue. Three things are worth keeping when you adapt it:

- **Every format goes back to the original.** Deriving format 8 from format 2 compounds the
  drift, and the twelfth piece ends up being about something the article never said.
- **The `Reject if` lines are the useful half of this file.** A rule that only says what to
  produce gets you twelve near-identical pieces. The rejection criterion is what forces them
  apart.
- **Keep a human on the queue.** Roughly one piece in ten lands the facts and misses the
  register, and register is the thing a model is worst at judging about its own output.
